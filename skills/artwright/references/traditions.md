# Traditions beyond the Western canon

Criteria from Chinese, Indian, Japanese and Islamic traditions, for judging a work by its own tradition's terms instead of a Western default.

## Borrowed-term guard

These words have exact meanings inside their traditions. When one is applied to a Western image, a game asset or an AI render, say it is a loose borrowed sense ("wabi-sabi in the popular sense: worn, asymmetric, muted"), or use plain English. SEP "Japanese Aesthetics" (revised 2023-12-06) presents *wabi* and *sabi* as two separate terms and does not use the compound "wabi-sabi"; it stresses that these ideas arose before aesthetics existed as a discipline, that their meanings changed over time (*mono no aware*, *sabi*), and that some do not translate (Kuki on *iki*: no European word carries it). Do not treat any of them as one timeless "East Asian" essence. Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/, fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes.

## China: Xie He's six principles

From the preface to Xie He's *Guhua pinlu* (6th c.), as glossed by Wikipedia (an article flagged for needing more citations); the translation of the first principle is debated in the scholarly literature (Acker, Soper, Cahill), not checked here.

| # | Term | Gloss | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *qiyun shengdong* | spirit resonance, vitality | the whole work carries energy; without it, Xie He said, there is no need to look further |
| 2 | *gufa yongbi* | bone method, use of the brush | stroke structure, pressure and speed; the link to calligraphy |
| 3 | *yingwu xiangxing* | correspondence to the object, depicting form | shape and line true to the thing |
| 4 | *suilei fucai* | suitability to type, applying colour | colour, layers and tone fit the subject |
| 5 | *jingying weizhi* | division and planning, placement | composition, space, depth, the empty areas |
| 6 | *chuanyi muxie* | transmission by copying | learning from earlier models; copying was a virtue, not a fault |

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xie_He_(artist), fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes for the list; no for the translation debate.

SEP's 2026 entry on Chinese painting and calligraphy does not cover Xie He, but adds: literati painting is "the only form of painting theorized in the Chinese tradition" and values *xieyi* ("writing intention"), suggestive painting built on brushwork over colour; Jing Hao (10th c.) set formal resemblance (*si*) against truth (*zhen*). *Ask of a literati work: is the brushwork alive and is the incompleteness deliberate?* Do not mark it down for "unfinished" areas or the lack of a single light source. Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-painting-calligraphy/ (first published 2026-02-25), fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes.

## South Asia: rasa

Rasa ("juice, essence, taste") is the emotional flavour a work evokes in a responsive viewer (*sahṛdaya*). The *Natyashastra*, attributed to Bharata, has a chapter on it (Chapter 6); its compilation is dated about 200 BCE–200 CE, with estimates from 500 BCE to 500 CE. Its fullest account is by Abhinavagupta (c. 1000 CE). Sources: Wikipedia "Rasa (aesthetics)" (Q620563) and "Natya Shastra" (Q550599), fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes for these points.

The eight rasas: *śṛṅgāra* (love), *hāsya* (comic), *karuṇa* (pathos), *raudra* (fury), *vīra* (heroic), *bhayānaka* (terror), *bībhatsa* (disgust), *adbhuta* (wonder); a ninth, *śānta* (peace), was added later, with its first proponent disputed. Rasa arises from determinants (*vibhāva*), consequents (*anubhāva*) and transitory states (*vyabhicāri bhāva*) acting on a stable emotion (*sthāyi bhāva*). Source: creation pass report A, F13, from secondary sources. v: no; a scholarly reference is still owed (R-20261021-20).

*Ask of a Rajput or Pahari painting: which rasa is it built for (a Krishna love scene is usually śṛṅgāra), and do pose, colour and setting serve it?* The Shilpa Shastras (proportion by *tāla* units for sacred images) are not yet researched. v: no.

## Japan: terms

SEP's six terms, with its glosses: *mono no aware* (the pathos of things, from their transience), *wabi* (subdued, austere beauty), *sabi* (rustic patina; it once meant desolateness, later something aged well), *yūgen* (mysterious profundity), *iki* (refined style), *kire* (cutting, and the continuity across a cut). Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/, fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes.

*Ma* (negative space; literally "gap, space, pause") is not treated by SEP; Wikipedia "Ma (negative space)" (Q3273419), fetched 2026-10-21, is a thin source. v: yes for the gloss only. *Ask: is the empty area working as an interval, or is it simply unfilled?*

## Islamic world: geometry and calligraphy

- **Geometric pattern.** Patterns grew from simple stars and lozenges in the 9th century, through 6- to 13-point patterns by the 13th, to 14- and 16-point stars by the 16th. *Girih* ("knot") is interlaced strapwork from about 1000 CE, flourishing to the 15th century, built with straightedge and compass, a polygon grid or a set of girih tiles (the 15th-century Topkapı Scroll shows patterns with their tilings). There is no evidence that medieval makers used aperiodic Penrose-type sets. *Check: a true repeat (or a deliberate quasi-periodic one), stars of a consistent order, strapwork that passes over and under consistently, no broken lines at tile joins.* Sources: Wikipedia "Islamic geometric patterns" (Q3325173) and "Girih" (Q17050256), fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes.
- **Calligraphy.** *Khatt* ("line, design") covers Arabic, Persian, Ottoman and Urdu scripts; Kufic is said to have developed by the late 7th century in Kufa, later in floral, foliated, plaited and square forms. Rounded scripts (*naskh*, *thuluth*, *nastaʿlīq*) are not covered by the fetched source. *Check: letterforms consistent in one script; baselines and proportions held; text legible and not pseudo-script.* AI images often produce pseudo-Arabic; say so plainly, and never call it calligraphy. Source: Wikipedia "Islamic calligraphy" (Q656831), fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes for the history; no for the AI pseudo-script note (an observation, not sourced).
- **Figures.** The Quran does not explicitly forbid images of people and animals, but religious contexts often avoided them; Persian, Mughal and Ottoman manuscript painting is figural (see [movements.md](movements.md)). Same source. v: yes.
