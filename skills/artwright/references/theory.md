# Theory waves

The main waves of thinking about art, each turned into a question the agent can ask of an image, with a source to read further.

## The waves

**Mimesis (Plato, Aristotle).** Art as imitation; Plato distrusts it as a copy of a copy, Aristotle finds knowledge and pleasure in it. SEP notes "imitation" is serviceable but does not carry everything *mimēsis* means. *Ask: what does the image imitate, and what does the imitation get right or leave out?* Slugs `plato-aesthetics`, `aristotle-aesthetics`. v: yes.

**Kant's judgement of taste.** A judgement of beauty is disinterested (not about wanting the thing), claims everyone's agreement, yet rests on feeling rather than a concept. *Ask: am I responding to the form, or to liking the subject, the brand or the model that made it?* Slug `kant-aesthetics`. v: no (slug checked, content not re-read).

**Romantic expression.** Art as the expression of feeling and imagination (German early Romanticism after Kant); later Collingwood: expression clarifies an emotion, while craft that only arouses a set emotion is not art proper. *Ask: what feeling is here, and is it worked out in the image or only signalled by stock cues?* Slugs `aesthetics-19th-romantic`, `collingwood-aesthetics`. v: yes for the Romantic entry; no for the Collingwood summary.

**Formalism (Bell, Fry, Greenberg).** Value lies in form: Bell's "significant form"; Greenberg's modernist painting that stresses flatness and its own medium. *Ask: what do line, shape, colour and value do on their own, with the subject ignored?* Slugs `aesthetic-concept` (discusses Bell and Greenberg), `art-definition` (formalist definitions). v: yes for SEP coverage; the Greenberg gloss is general knowledge.

**Iconology (Panofsky).** Three levels: pre-iconographic description (what is shown), iconography (conventional meaning: a woman with a wheel is St Catherine), iconology (what the work reveals of its culture). Tate: "the iconography of an artwork is the imagery within it." *Ask: what is shown, what does it conventionally mean, and what does it say about when and where it was made?* Slug `art-interpretation`; https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/i/iconography. v: no for Panofsky's levels (not in the fetched pages).

**Social art history (Clark, Baxandall; critical theory).** Art as made under social and economic conditions; Baxandall's "period eye" asks what skills a viewer of the time brought; the Frankfurt School asks about commodification and popular culture. *Ask: who paid for this, who was meant to see it, and what does it hide?* Slug `aesthetics-critical-theory` (Adorno, Marcuse, Jameson). v: yes for the critical-theory entry; no for Clark and Baxandall.

**Danto's artworld.** An artwork and an indiscernible ordinary object can look the same; what makes one art is an atmosphere of theory and history. *Ask: if this image had a visually identical twin that was not art, what would separate them?* Slug `art-definition`. v: yes (Danto 1964 and 1981 in SEP).

**Dickie's institutional theory.** "An artifact of a kind created, by an artist, to be presented to an artworld public" (Dickie 1984, quoted by SEP). *Ask: is it made and offered as art, and to which public?* Slug `art-definition`. v: yes.

**Weitz's open concept.** "Art" is an open concept with no necessary and sufficient conditions; new cases call for a decision to extend it (Weitz 1956). *Ask: does this case extend the concept, and what earlier cases does it resemble?* Slug `art-definition`. v: yes.

**Gaut's cluster account.** No single property is required; art is marked by a cluster (positive aesthetic qualities, expressed emotion, intellectual challenge, formal complexity, meaning, an individual point of view, originality, skill, membership of an art form, intent to make art) (Gaut 2000). *Ask: how many of the cluster does this meet, and which are missing?* Slug `art-definition`. v: yes for the cluster view; the ten-item list is from general knowledge.

**Krauss and Owens (October critics).** Krauss's "expanded field" (1979) and the grid; Owens's "allegorical impulse" (1980): appropriation, site-specificity and layered images as postmodern strategies. *Ask: does the image quote or layer other images, and to what end?* No SEP entry; see the postmodernism section below. v: no.

**Feminist art history and aesthetics.** Nochlin's "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" moved the question from talent to institutions; later work on the gaze and gendered genres. *Ask: who is looking, who is looked at, and which genres or materials were ranked low?* Slug `feminism-aesthetics`. v: yes.

**Postcolonial critique and "primitivism".** Aesthetics was "implicated in colonial hierarchies" and keeps needing readings that probe its Eurocentrism (SEP, new entry 2026-09-22). The primitivism critique (notably of MoMA's 1984 "Primitivism" show) objects to non-Western objects shown as anonymous, timeless sources for Western modernists. Tate has withdrawn its own "primitivism" glossary text as outdated and is rewriting it (fetched 2026-10-21). *Ask: whose forms are borrowed, are they named and dated, and whose criteria judge them?* Slugs `aesthetics-race`, `colonialism`; https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/p/primitivism. v: yes for SEP and Tate; no for the MoMA 1984 details.

**Relational aesthetics and its antagonist (Bourriaud, Bishop).** Bourriaud's *Relational Aesthetics* (1998): art "based on, or inspired by, human relations and their social context" (Tate; Parreno, Gillick, Gordon, Wearing). Bishop (2004) answered that the relations were too comfortable and argued for antagonism. *Ask: what social situation does the work make, and who is left out of it?* https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/r/relational-aesthetics. v: yes for Bourriaud via Tate; no for Bishop.

**Post-internet.** Art made when the internet is no longer a novelty; the term "postinternet art" is credited to Marisa Olson (2006) (Wikipedia lead, Q17299972). *Ask: does the image treat screens, stock imagery and circulation as its material?* Slug `digital-art`. v: yes.

**AI authorship.** SEP's digital-art entry treats AI art as a test case for creativity and collaboration: credit usually goes to whoever initiates and monitors the program, while some artists call the system a "partner". In US law human authorship is required (Thaler cert denied 2026-03-02; R-20261021-10). *Ask: which decisions in this image show a human choice (brief, selection, edit), and which are model defaults?* Slug `digital-art`. v: yes.

## Gold theory answers

**Neo-expressionism.** A revival of expressionist painting that emerged in the late 1970s and peaked internationally in the 1980s: crude figuration, thick paint, big canvases, history and myth. It was "a reaction to the minimalism and conceptual art that had dominated the 1970s" (Tate). Names: Georg Baselitz, Anselm Kiefer, Julian Schnabel, David Salle (Tate); Germany's *Neue Wilden*, Italy's *Transavanguardia* (with Francesco Clemente), France's *Figuration Libre* (1981). Jean-Michel Basquiat is commonly grouped with it (not in the fetched pages). Sources: https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/n/neo-expressionism; Wikipedia lead "Neo-expressionism" (Q589655: "emerged in the late 1970s ... as a reaction against conceptual art and minimal art"), both fetched 2026-10-21. v: yes, except Basquiat and Clemente as named members.

**Postmodernism.** Tate: a term "used from about 1970 to describe changes seen to take place in Western society and culture from the 1960s onwards", a reaction against modernism's idealism, belief in progress and universal truths; in art it "may be said to begin with pop art in the 1960s" and to take in conceptual art, neo-expressionism, feminist art and the YBAs. In philosophy SEP centres it on Lyotard's incredulity toward grand narratives (slug `postmodernism`, last revised 2015). Visible signs to check: quotation and pastiche, mixed high and low sources, irony, text with image. Sources: https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/p/postmodernism, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/postmodernism/, fetched 2026-10-21. MoMA's term pages returned 403. v: yes.

Wider reading for the twentieth century: *Art Since 1900* (Foster, Krauss, Bois, Buchloh, Joselit), cited by R-20261021-20; not fetched.
