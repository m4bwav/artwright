## Bodies

Body type from a photograph is a guess. The tests that settle it need the object: a chip or the unglazed foot, light through a thin edge, the sound when tapped. Classes overlap, and composition, firing temperature and water absorption all count.

| Body | Traits | Typical firing | verified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earthenware | Porous unless glazed, opaque, buff to red; tin-glazed wares (Delft, maiolica) show the coloured body at chips | about 926-1150 °C | yes: firing from the Science Museum Group (collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co418180); traits B-F15 |
| Stoneware | Vitrified, near watertight, opaque, often grey or buff; salt glaze gives an orange-peel surface | about 1205-1260 °C (other sources to 1300) | yes, same source; traits B-F15 |
| Porcelain | Kaolin-based, vitrified, white through the cross-section, translucent at thin edges (translucency alone is not proof; hotel porcelain fails it), rings when tapped | kaolin vitrifies at about 1280-1400 °C | yes, same source and B-F15 |

## Glazes and firing

| Term | Meaning | verified |
|---|---|---|
| Biscuit (bisque) | First firing of unglazed clay | no |
| Oxidation and reduction | Kiln atmosphere with or without spare oxygen; reduction turns iron glazes green-blue (celadon) and copper red | no |
| Celadon | Iron-bearing glaze fired in reduction, grey-green to blue-green | no |
| Underglaze blue | Cobalt painted on the body, covered with clear glaze, one high firing: blue-and-white | yes (Cleveland 1957.61 record describes the process) |
| Underglaze red | Copper under the glaze; hard to control, often greyish or blurred | no |
| Overglaze enamels | Colours painted on the fired glaze and fixed in a second, lower firing (wucai, doucai, famille verte, famille rose) | yes for doucai (Cleveland 1957.61) |
| Doucai ("joined colours") | Outlines in underglaze blue, filled with overglaze enamels after the first firing | yes, Cleveland 1957.61 |
| Crackle | Deliberate crazing from glaze and body shrinking at different rates (Ge ware) | no |
| Tin glaze | Opaque white glaze over earthenware, a ground for painting | no |
| Salt glaze | Salt thrown into the kiln reacts with the body | no |

## Decoration methods

Painting under or over the glaze; incising and carving; moulding and sprigging (applied relief); slip trailing and sgraffito (scratching through slip); piercing (reticulated); resist. verified: no

"Ming vase" in speech often means any Chinese vase. Keep Yuan, Ming, Qing and Republican apart: the auction records named under "Ming" are often Qing (see the market reference and R-20261021-14).

## Reign marks

- A six-character mark reads in columns, top to bottom and right to left: two characters for the dynasty (da Ming, da Qing), two for the reign, then *nianzhi*, "made in the period". Four-character marks drop the dynasty; *yuzhi* means made by imperial order. verified: yes for the structure (aziatischekeramiek.nl); the reading order from B-F15, no
- Script: regular (*kaishu*) or archaic seal (*zhuanshu*). Painted in underglaze blue or overglaze (iron red, enamel), sometimes moulded or stamped. verified: yes (aziatischekeramiek.nl)
- Marks first appear in the Ming dynasty and are regular on imperial wares from Xuande (1426-1435). A few Yongle wares carry a four-character seal mark. verified: yes for Ming and Xuande; Yongle no
- **Apocryphal marks**: Kangxi potters put Ming marks (Xuande, Chenghua) on new wares as homage, not as forgery. Chenghua is probably the most copied mark. Later copies and fakes add Qing marks too. verified: yes (aziatischekeramiek.nl)

**The rule: a reign mark never dates a piece alone.** Date by the whole object (body, glaze, foot, potting, painting style, wear) and say which phrase applies: "mark and period" (the mark agrees with the object), or "mark of X, not of the period". From a photograph, say what the mark claims, what the visible traits suggest, and that confirming it needs the object or a catalogue record. Never authenticate or value from pixels (R-20261021-21).

## Judging a ware by its own criteria

Do not judge a Chinese cup by Western painting rules (thirds, perspective). Judge it as its makers and collectors did:

- **Body and potting**: thinness, evenness, weight in the hand, a clean foot ring.
- **Glaze**: colour, depth and evenness; bubbles, pooling and pinholes; for blue-and-white, the tone of the cobalt (heaped and blotchy in early Ming, soft and even in Chenghua).
- **Painting**: line quality, how motifs fill the curved surface, enamel colours sitting inside their outlines.
- **Form**: proportion of profile and foot, how the rim turns.
- **Mark**: style and calligraphy compared with the object, never on its own.

verified: no (connoisseurship convention), except the worked example below.

**Worked example**: Cleveland Museum of Art, *Wine Cup with Children at Play* (1957.61, CC0), "Ming dynasty (1368-1644), Chenghua mark and period (1465-1487)", doucai. The museum praises "free, spontaneous drawing as well as perfect compositional balance over its small surface area" and a body "thin, translucent, and light in weight". A good answer names Ming and the Chenghua reign, explains doucai (blue outlines, glaze firing, enamels, second firing), judges drawing, balance on a curved surface, body and enamel fit, and treats the mark as consistent with the period on the museum's authority, not as proof by itself. verified: yes (openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1957.61, read 2026-10-21)
